Noise City

In which a reasonable man reaches his limit.

Meet Mr. Bapat

Mr. Bapat is forty-one, an engineer, and until quite recently a reasonable man. He pays his taxes. He returns his neighbours' parcels. He has never once raised his voice, largely because for the past three weeks nobody would have heard him.

He has nothing against festivals. He likes festivals. He has contributed generously to at least four of them, one of which he is fairly sure was made up. What he objects to is a wall of speakers roughly the height of his flat, parked directly beneath his bedroom window, playing a remix of a remix of a song he did not enjoy the first time.

The reasonable phase

He tried the correct channels. He asked politely. He asked less politely. He rang the number the municipal corporation publishes for exactly this purpose, and a recorded voice thanked him warmly for his call and hung up. At some point during all this, the DJ — sensing weakness — added a second speaker wall.

The dhol pathak arrives at seven in the morning. The sound wall takes the night shift. Between them they have achieved full coverage. Mr. Bapat has not slept properly since the eleventh.

The unreasonable phase

At 1:47 a.m. on a Tuesday — the third consecutive Tuesday, he had been counting — Mr. Bapat put on his shoes, took the stick from behind the door, and went downstairs to have a word with the equipment.

Nobody gets hurt in Noise City. Mr. Bapat is not that sort of man. Only amplifiers.

How it works

Every speaker, screen and drum has a decibel reading, and that reading is its health. Hit it and the number falls — and so does the actual volume coming out of your device, until the street is silent. Meanwhile the noise is wearing you down: sound rings drain your Calm, so standing around listening is the one thing you cannot afford to do.
